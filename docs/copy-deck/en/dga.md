## 02 — How the phenomenon works

**Eyebrow:** 01 — THE PHENOMENON

**Headline:** The oil keeps a record of what happens inside the tank.

**Body:**
A transformer's insulation is oil and paper. Under normal operation both age slowly. Under abnormal stress, whether electrical or thermal, they decompose faster and release gases. Those gases dissolve in the oil and circulate with it.

Which gases form depends on how much energy the fault releases. Low-energy processes produce mainly hydrogen. Very high temperatures and arcing produce acetylene. Other gases sit in between, and their proportions are what a laboratory uses to tell fault types apart.

The oil therefore carries information that cannot be seen from outside the tank. A laboratory sample reads it in detail, at intervals. An online monitor reads part of it, all the time.

**Pull-quote:**
A laboratory sample tells you a great deal, occasionally. A monitor tells you less, continuously. You need both.

## 04 — How to read the measurement

**Eyebrow:** 03 — READING IT

**Headline:** The rate matters more than the number.

**Body blocks**

- **Level.** The concentration of a gas in the oil, in parts per million. A level means little without the transformer's own history: design, age, loading and earlier samples all shift what is normal for a unit.
- **Rate of change.** How fast the level is rising. A stable level, even a raised one, describes a past event or a stable condition. A rising level describes something active now. This is what an online monitor shows and a periodic sample cannot.
- **Hydrogen as the general signal.** Because most fault types produce it, hydrogen is the broad alert. It says "look closer", not "this is the cause".
- **Acetylene as the serious signal.** It forms only at very high energy. In a transformer where it has been absent, its appearance deserves prompt attention.
- **Moisture with temperature.** Water moves between paper and oil as temperature changes. A moisture reading is therefore read together with oil temperature and load, and its trend over days says more than one value.

**What it can and cannot tell you (honesty block)**

| A two-gas online monitor can tell you | It cannot tell you |
|---|---|
| That gas is being generated, and how quickly | The type of fault: ratio-based methods need the full set of gases |
| That acetylene has appeared or is rising | Where in the transformer the fault is |
| That moisture in the oil is rising | The condition of the paper insulation directly |
| When to take an oil sample sooner than planned | Whether the unit is safe to keep in service; that is an engineering decision made with more data |

**Glossary one-liners**
- **DGA** — dissolved gas analysis: measuring the gases dissolved in transformer oil.
- **ppm** — parts per million, the unit of gas concentration in oil.
- **Limit of detection** — the smallest concentration the instrument can report.
- **Multi-gas / full DGA** — laboratory analysis of the full set of fault gases, used for fault-type interpretation.
- **Ratio methods** — interpretation methods that compare the proportions of several gases. They cannot be applied to two gases alone.

## 07 — What happens after an alarm

**Eyebrow:** 06 — AFTER AN ALARM

**Headline:** The monitor raises the question. The laboratory answers it.

1. **Alarm.** Hydrogen, acetylene or moisture passes a set limit, or rises faster than a set rate.
2. **Check the context.** Load, oil temperature, recent switching or maintenance. Rule out an operational explanation.
3. **Take an oil sample.** Send it for full multi-gas laboratory DGA.
4. **Interpret.** The laboratory result is interpreted under IEC 60599 or IEEE C57.104. This is the step that points to a fault type.
5. **Cross-check and plan.** Compare with partial discharge data and electrical tests. Decide on loading, further tests or an outage.

**Caption:** General sequence. Limits and procedures are set by the asset owner.

## 10 — FAQ

**Q: Is InsuLogix G2 a full DGA monitor?**
No. It measures two gases, hydrogen and acetylene, and moisture. It is an early-warning monitor. Fault-type diagnosis needs laboratory multi-gas DGA.

**Q: Then why monitor only two gases?**
Because they carry most of the early-warning information. Hydrogen appears in most developing faults. Acetylene points to high-energy faults. Together they say when to look closer.

**Q: Can we stop taking laboratory samples?**
No. The monitor fills the time between samples and tells you when to take one sooner. Routine laboratory analysis remains the basis for diagnosis.

**Q: What does a moisture alarm mean?**
That water content in the oil has risen. Moisture lowers the dielectric strength of the oil and speeds up paper ageing. Read it with oil temperature, because water moves between paper and oil as temperature changes.

**Q: How does the monitor connect to our systems?**
Per manufacturer data: Modbus RTU and Modbus TCP as standard, DNP3 and IEC 61850 as options.
