## 02 — Why continuous monitoring

**Eyebrow:** 01 — WHY CONTINUOUS

**Headline:** The asset looks the same on the day before it fails.

**Body:**
Most insulation and network faults do not appear suddenly. They develop over weeks or months, and for most of that time the asset keeps operating normally. A periodic test records one moment; a fault that starts the week after goes unseen until the next test. A permanently installed monitor measures while the asset is in service, records the trend and raises an alarm when a set limit is passed. Maintenance can then be planned around the condition of the asset instead of a fixed calendar or an unplanned outage.

**Three short blocks (scannable)**

- **Measure in service.** No outage is needed to take a reading. The asset is observed under its real load, temperature and voltage.
- **Record the trend.** One value says little. The direction and the rate of change say whether to act.
- **Alarm on a limit.** A set limit turns a slow change into a clear moment for a decision, while there is still time to plan.

**Pull-quote:**
A single reading tells you where you are. A trend tells you where you are going.

**What monitoring does not do (honesty block)**
- It does not repair anything. It gives time and information.
- It does not replace offline testing. An alarm is usually followed by a targeted test or a laboratory sample to confirm the finding.
- It does not see every failure mode. Each method watches specific phenomena, which is why the table below maps methods to assets.

**Glossary one-liners**
- **Online** — measured while the asset is energised and in service.
- **Continuous** — measured permanently, not at intervals.
- **Trend** — how a measured value changes over time.
- **Alarm limit** — a set value which, when passed, triggers a notification.
- **Condition-based maintenance** — work planned from the measured state of the asset, not from the calendar.

## 05 — From alarm to decision (short process strip)

**Eyebrow:** 04 — AFTER AN ALARM

**Headline:** An alarm is the start of a decision, not the end of one.

1. **Alarm.** A trend passes its set limit. The asset is still in service.
2. **Confirm.** Check the reading against load, temperature and other measurements. Take a targeted test or an oil sample where needed.
3. **Assess.** Decide how fast the condition is changing and what is at stake if it continues.
4. **Plan.** Schedule the intervention, the outage and the materials at a time you choose.

**Caption:** General sequence. The actual procedure is the operator's and depends on the asset and its criticality.

## 07 — FAQ

**Q: Does online monitoring replace periodic testing?**
No. The two work together. Monitoring watches continuously for specific changes. Offline tests and laboratory analysis go deeper when a change needs to be confirmed or explained.

**Q: Does the asset have to be taken out of service to be monitored?**
Monitoring itself runs while the asset is in service; that is its purpose. Whether an outage is needed to install sensors depends on the asset and the sensor type, and is defined for each installation.

**Q: Which assets should be monitored first?**
Usually those where a failure costs most and a replacement takes longest. For most networks that starts with power transformers. The "what is different" column in the table is a good guide.

**Q: Can existing sensors be used?**
It depends on the system and the sensor. For partial discharge, see the ICMobserver section on the Partial Discharge Monitoring page.
