## Article body

### Nobody looks at a transformer

A building has lighting, lifts, screens and air conditioning. People notice all of them. Behind them stands a transformer in a locked room or a fenced yard, and almost nobody has seen it. The cable that feeds it is under the ground. The switchgear is in a steel enclosure. This is how it should be. Good infrastructure stays out of the way.

The difficulty is that it stays out of the way of its owners as well. Electrical infrastructure is noticed on one occasion: when it stops. On every other day it is silent, enclosed and assumed.

### Why condition stays out of sight

Three properties of electrical equipment combine to hide its condition.

**The output does not change.** A pump that wears delivers less flow. A bearing that wears makes noise. A transformer with deteriorating insulation delivers exactly the same voltage as a new one. So does a cable with a weak joint. The quantity the operator watches, the supply, says nothing about the state of the equipment that supplies it. Until the insulation fails, performance is perfect.

**The process is slow.** Most insulation and network faults do not appear suddenly. They develop over weeks or months, and for most of that time the asset keeps operating normally. A small void discharges a little on each cycle. Oil takes up moisture season by season. A joint runs slightly warmer each summer. None of these produces an event. Each produces a drift.

**The equipment is closed.** The parts that matter are inside a tank of oil, inside a sealed enclosure, inside the stator of a machine or under a road. They cannot be inspected by eye, and opening them is an outage in itself.

Put together: a slow process, inside a closed box, with no effect on the output. That is what invisible means here. It is not that the information does not exist. It is that nothing in daily operation brings it to the surface.

### What a periodic test sees

The traditional answer is the periodic test. The asset is taken out of service, measured, and returned. This has real value. Offline tests are thorough, they are made under controlled conditions, and they give a reference for everything that follows.

But a periodic test records one moment. If the result is good, the report is correct about that day. A fault that starts the week after goes unseen until the next test. Whether it is found in time depends on whether it develops more slowly than the test interval. Sometimes it does. The interval was chosen for the calendar, not for the fault.

There is a second limit. A test needs an outage, and outages are hard to obtain. The more critical the asset, the less willing the operation is to release it. So the assets that matter most are often the ones tested least.

### What continuous measurement makes visible

A deteriorating asset does give signs. They are simply not signs an operator sees without instruments. Insulation defects produce partial discharge. Faults inside an oil-filled transformer release gases into the oil. Oil and paper take up moisture. A cable joint in trouble shows it in temperature and in the current in its screen. A disturbed network shows it in the shape of the voltage.

A permanently installed monitor measures such a quantity while the asset is in service, records the trend and raises an alarm when a set limit is passed. Three things change as a result.

First, the unit of information changes from a point to a line. One reading says where the asset is. A trend says where it is going and how fast. For some assets this is the only meaningful form of the information: a generator always has some partial discharge in normal operation, so the single reading means little and the change means a great deal.

Second, the timing changes. The finding arrives while the asset is still in service. The outage can be planned, the materials ordered and the work done at a time the owner chooses. For a power transformer, where a replacement takes a long time to obtain, this is where early warning has its highest value.

Third, the basis for maintenance changes. Work can be planned around the condition of the asset instead of a fixed calendar or an unplanned outage.

### What it does not make visible

It is worth being exact about the limits, because monitoring is sometimes sold as certainty.

A monitor sees what it is built to see. A partial discharge monitor sees discharge activity; it says nothing about a failure mode that produces none. A two-gas monitor shows that gas is being generated and how quickly; identifying the type of fault needs a full laboratory analysis. A power quality analyser describes the voltage at the point where it is installed, not elsewhere.

No monitor gives a failure date. What it gives is a change, and time to investigate it. An alarm is normally the start of further work: a sample, a targeted test, an inspection.

And monitoring does not replace judgement. Someone has to set the limits, read the trend against load and temperature, and decide. Continuous measurement moves the decision earlier. It does not make it.

### From one asset to the whole fleet

Visibility of one asset is useful. But the owner of a network has hundreds or thousands of assets and a limited budget. The real risk at that scale is maintenance and investment effort placed on the wrong assets: replacing by age a unit that was sound, while another fails early.

This is where the separate readings have to come together. Asset performance management software adds no new measurement. It collects test and monitoring data into one record per asset and ranks the fleet by condition and by importance. Its value depends entirely on the quality of the data beneath it, which is one more reason to make that data continuous where it matters.

### Making it visible

Infrastructure should stay out of the way of the people it serves. It should not stay out of sight of the people responsible for it. The condition of a transformer, a cable or a machine can be known while it runs. Not completely, and not with certainty, but well enough to turn a surprise into a plan.

That is the whole of the argument. Faults take months. Outages take a moment. The months are when something can be done.
